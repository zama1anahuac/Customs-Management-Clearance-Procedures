13.1

Tariff Classification (HS Code) & Customs Valuation

Regina Espadas

Tariff classification groups goods into categories under the Harmonized Tariff Schedule or similar frameworks. The category assigned determines the applicable duties and regulatory requirements, and it can be managed strategically through redesign, repackaging or shipping choices to optimize duty savings, as seen in US–European Community trade relations.

Customs valuation sets the value of imported goods for duty assessment. The WTO agreement promotes a fair, uniform and neutral system that prohibits arbitrary or fictitious values.

Transaction value (TV)The preferred method: the price actually paid or payable when goods are sold for export, with statutory adjustments. Requires a sale for export.
Hierarchy of methodsWhen TV doesn't apply, other methods follow in strict order so duties rest on objective, verifiable criteria.
ChallengeDeveloping countries often struggle to implement these methods, reducing customs efficiency.
Classification determines which duties apply; valuation determines the amount they apply to. Both require careful planning and compliance.

13.2

Customs Clearance Process & Non-Tariff Regulations (RRNAs)

Zama

Even as trade agreements lower tariffs, administrative barriers and regulatory requirements still shape trade costs and competitiveness. Clearance involves documentation, tariff classification, valuation, origin verification and compliance with import and export rules. Excessive paperwork, complicated licensing and inefficient practices cause delays and raise costs.

In Mexico, RRNAs include sanitary and phytosanitary measures, technical standards, product certifications, labelling requirements and import permits. They protect health, consumers, the environment and security, but inconsistent implementation can hurt businesses, especially in developing economies.

Across 60 countries (data from 2007, 2010 and 2012), customs efficiency showed a positive, statistically significant relationship with both exports and imports (Gani, 2015).

Improving efficiency requires regulatory transparency, simpler procedures, stronger institutional coordination and effective compliance mechanisms.

13.3

Preventative Customs Compliance & Risk Audits

Team 13

Preventative compliance is the set of policies, procedures and internal controls that prevent errors in international trade. It covers tariff classification, valuation, rules of origin, record-keeping and reliable audit trails, so that declarations are accurate and fully documented.

Risk-based managementAuthorities use risk indicators and selectivity systems to focus on higher-risk transactions instead of inspecting every shipment.
Post-clearance auditsReview of records, declarations and supporting documents after goods are released.
Data analyticsDetects unusual values, inconsistent declarations and recurring error patterns.
Compliance isn't only about detecting violations: it should encourage voluntary compliance, improve internal procedures and facilitate legitimate trade.

13.4

Digital Customs Clearance Integration (VUCEM & ANAM)

Diego

Digital clearance replaces paper-based processes with electronic documents and data exchange. It reduces paperwork and manual entry, improves accuracy, lowers administrative costs and improves transparency and communication among importers, exporters, brokers, carriers and authorities.

Single WindowOne centralized platform to submit standardized information once instead of separately to many agencies.
VUCEMMexico's Ventanilla Única de Comercio Exterior, supporting electronic processing of foreign-trade documents.
ANAMAgencia Nacional de Aduanas de México, responsible for customs administration and enforcement.

Success needs more than technology: legal recognition of electronic documents, secure systems, standardized data, coordination between agencies, and training and cooperation among public and private actors.

Case study: Chile

A study of 15 semi-structured interviews with customs agents found that the State had to involve agents in implementing its single window because of their expertise. Reactions ranged from apprehension to active collaboration. A major weakness was the lack of participation by other government agencies, which undermined the goal of connecting them at a single point.

Official portal: ventanillaunica.gob.mx (manuals, procedures, electronic documents, permits)

13.5

Administrative Customs Procedures (PAMA) & Defense Strategies

Said Burgos

PAMA (Procedimiento Administrativo en Materia Aduanera) is the legal process Mexican customs authorities use when they detect possible irregularities, such as missing documents, failure to prove legal import, or noncompliance. Merchandise may be temporarily seized during the investigation.

Landavazo Moreno and Zalazar Zavala (2025) identify problems in practice: delayed notifications, inadequate evaluation of evidence, insufficient training and informal practices. These create legal uncertainty, financial losses and business interruptions, especially when goods stay seized for long periods.

Defense strategies

DocumentationKeep complete import documentation.
ComplianceVerify compliance with customs requirements.
DeadlinesRespond to notifications within established deadlines.
Legal helpSeek customs specialists and challenge actions when authorities don't follow legal requirements.
Under Article 150 of Mexico's Customs Law, the person concerned generally has ten business days after notification takes effect to present evidence and arguments, subject to applicable provisions.
